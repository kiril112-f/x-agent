# Concept before decoration

Kirill's preference: an illustration can reflect the article philosophically, indirectly or through exaggeration. It does not need to show a phone just because the article concerns an app. It must still carry a specific relationship from the article. A random futuristic scene is not conceptual depth.

## Build the visual sentence

Read the article or its actual thesis first. Write four short internal lines:

1. **Literal thesis:** what the reader should understand or do.
2. **Tension:** what prevents that outcome or what changes.
3. **Visible relationship:** who/what acts on what, with which visible consequence.
4. **Mapping:** which element stands for which part of the thesis.

Then sketch three different mechanisms in words, not three palettes of the same picture. Usually one literal option, one indirect metaphor and one more philosophical option are enough to make a decision. Choose the one whose meaning survives both a thumbnail and a one-sentence explanation.

### Tested examples

| Article mechanism | Visual sentence | Why the mapping works | What would break it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stop hiding work until it feels perfect | A person pulls back an enormous curtain and reveals a horizon | Deliberate disclosure changes what can be seen | A generic mountain landscape with no action would become motivational wallpaper |
| Build a repeatable content operation | One operator turns a press that produces a coherent stream of paper | One repeatable input becomes repeated output | Random gears, money piles, extra machines with no visible connection |
| Design distribution rather than only production | A carefully folded plane carries the possibility of movement | Making the object includes making it able to travel | A luxury car or spaceship chosen only because it looks expensive |
| Human attention must be earned | One ordinary engraved eye breaks through layers of torn paper | The viewer/reader is the focal actor, not an algorithm badge | Extra eyes, robot faces, unrelated circuitry or fabricated analytics |
| Choose a route to the audience | A continuous path links idea, format and audience | The diagram literally encodes the mechanism | Decorative nodes with no causal relation or tiny unreadable labels |

These are examples, not a stock metaphor bank. Reusing the same curtain for every self-development piece would lose specificity. Change the relationship when the thesis changes.

## Philosophy without hallucinated nonsense

Deliberate impossible scale is allowed: a person may pull a landscape-sized curtain. A visual metaphor is not a documentary claim. **Local coherence remains required:** the hand meets the cloth, feet meet the ledge, the fold responds to the pull, the light comes from one plausible region. A giant curtain is intentional; an extra arm, disconnected hand or object with no support is an accident.

Choose one symbolic departure, not five incompatible surreal rules. If the idea needs a paragraph of explanation before the image means anything, simplify the scene. Avoid loading every noun from the article into the image.

For monochrome engraving, use two or three large value masses before microtexture. Place the figure/action where its silhouette separates from the background. Dense crosshatching is a material treatment, not the main subject. Examine the 340 px image: if only noise remains, enlarge the figure or simplify the local background.

## Cold read

Look at the image without consulting the prompt. State what action or tension is visible. Compare it to the thesis. If they diverge, change the concept rather than inventing an explanation to defend the picture. Ask whether removing the title leaves a purposeful image. For art-led philosophical covers this is especially important.

Keep one line of mapping in the brief so another agent knows why the metaphor was chosen. Do not place that explanation on the public cover by default.
